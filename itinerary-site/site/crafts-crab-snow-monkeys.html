Crafts, Crab & Snow Monkeys

Two wildlife days on one rail line, crab week, and Kenrokuen at peak

Four regions, the best train seat in Japan, and a set of alignments that only work in this exact fortnight: Kenrokuen at its mid-November peak and snow crab season opening the week before you arrive. Trimming the craft workshops changed what this trip is about — it used to be the densest craft run of the five, and now its real distinction is wildlife. The Hokuriku line happens to thread both Karuizawa and Nagano, so the flying squirrels and the snow monkeys sit two days apart on the same rails. The trade is Kyoto — you arrive Nov 17–22, colour building but likely just before the forecast peak, in exchange for rooms about $300/night cheaper.

Rail + one car day

The Hokuriku shinkansen's Gran Class — attendant service, about $190 for the Tokyo→Kanazawa leg — is the best train seat in the country and exists only on this line. The route is partly built around it. The local Nagaden line plus a taxi covers the monkey park; no rental needed.

Kokuya

Shibu Onsen, Nagano·≈ $650 / night, half board

400 years old

A four-century-old ryokan in the wooden lanes of Shibu Onsen, and the reason Itinerary 2 sleeps here rather than in Nagano city. Flying squirrel watching, Karuizawa

Karuizawa, Nagano·≈ $47 / couple · 90 min

Wildlife>90% successSeason ends 30 Nov

The answer to “can we see a flying squirrel without going to Hokkaido.” Picchio has run this tour since 1995 — a briefing, then a walk to a known nest tree where a camera shows the animal inside grooming and stretching, and then, at dusk, it leaves the hole and glides into the forest. Karuizawa is 1 hour 10 minutes from Tokyo on the shinkansen.

Species

Owl café — Akiba Fukurou

Akihabara, Tokyo·≈ $45–50 / couple · 1 hr slot

Ashly asked for thisReservation only

Ashly asked to see an owl café, and this is the one worth booking. Akiba Fukurou runs reservation-only sessions with capped numbers and deliberately short hours, and says plainly that this is to prioritise the owls over throughput. Handling is offered rather than required — their FAQ says an owl may rest on your arm depending on how it is that day, and that “if an owl is resting, showing hesitation… we do not push.”

Why this one
It is the Tokyo owl venue that meets the criteria welfare reviewers apply: capped numbers, real rest, optional handling, stated veterinary care. Most Harajuku and Asakusa venues do not.
Booking
Reservation only via select-type.com, no walk-ins, 3 minutes from Akihabara. Card is charged at booking. Arrive 10 minutes early — the door is locked during the session and latecomers are not admitted.
What you get
A 1-hour slot; guests on the venue’s own review page report 40–45 minutes of actual owl time. Group sessions run about 10 people; private and VIP sessions are also sold. No food or drink. Photos allowed, no flash.
Price
Not published anywhere — it only appears inside the booking flow. Third-party reports cluster at ¥3,300–3,800 per person; treat the figure above as an estimate until you see the checkout.
See it for yourself
The photographs on this page are the venue’s own marketing shots — no independent photography of the room exists. Look closely and you can see what you are actually buying: the owls are tethered by cords to their perches and to guests’ arms, and the room is a single windowless-feeling interior with its one window curtained. That may be completely fine by you. It should just be a decision rather than a surprise.
The wider picture
Owl cafés as a category draw real criticism — a 2024 survey of 79 Japanese exotic-animal cafés found poor welfare scores across the board, mostly nocturnal animals kept awake in daylight under constant handling. Akiba Fukurou is the exception people point to, not the rule.
Sugar gliders — a problem
Not here; Akiba Fukurou is owls only. And both Tokyo venues that kept them have fallen over: Hagu Cafe in Shinjuku closed in June 2023, and Animal Room Ikemofu in Ikebukuro has lost its website — its domain now redirects to an auction parking page, having served normally as recently as March 2026. Third-party listings still show it, but nobody should plan around it without ringing ahead. The sugar glider photographs here are species references, not pictures of any café.

Where you actually are

The temples, gardens, lakes and landscapes this route passes through — shown in the season you would see them.

Kenrokuen

Kanazawa

One of the three great gardens of Japan, and it peaks in mid-November — precisely when Itinerary 2 arrives. The yukitsuri, the rope cones rigged over the pines to protect them from snow, go up in early November and are a sight in themselves.

Higashi Chaya

Kanazawa

A preserved teahouse district of wooden lattice fronts, and the centre of Japan’s gold leaf production — Kanazawa makes about 99% of it. Several teahouses open their interiors.

Zenko-ji

Nagano

A 7th-century temple that predates Japan’s split into Buddhist sects and therefore belongs to none of them. The main hall is one of the largest wooden buildings in the country. A natural stop between the shinkansen and Shibu Onsen.

Arashiyama

Western Kyoto

The bamboo grove, the Togetsukyo bridge under a turning mountainside, Tenryu-ji’s garden, and boats on the Hozugawa. It is also the natural stop on the way back from the Raku kiln at Kameoka.

Sanzen-in

Ohara, north Kyoto

A temple in a moss garden with small stone jizo figures half-hidden in the green, forty minutes north of the city. Ohara peaks slightly earlier than central Kyoto and is dramatically less crowded — which is why the indigo day is scheduled here.

Philosopher's Path & Honen-in

Northern Higashiyama, Kyoto

A canal walk under maples and cherries, with Honen-in’s thatched gate and raked sand a short detour off it. The itineraries send you at 8am for a reason — by ten in late November it is a queue.

Kodai-ji

Higashiyama, Kyoto

A temple with one of Kyoto’s best autumn night illuminations — the maples lit and mirrored in the Garyu-chi pond, plus a bamboo grove behind. Jugyuan, the two-star kaiseki restaurant, is immediately next door, so dinner and the illumination are one evening.

Gion

Kyoto

The wooden teahouse district you are staying inside. Hanamikoji at dusk, the Shirakawa canal, and the lane running up to the Yasaka pagoda — all within a short walk of Sowaka, which is the argument for staying there.

The verdict

This one changed the most. Its old selling point was being the densest craft itinerary, and that is gone — Mutoh kintsugi, the Raku kiln, the indigo and the Kutani/yuzen day all came out. What is left is arguably a better trip: three wildlife encounters (Karuizawa flying squirrels, Jigokudani macaques, Iwatayama macaques), crab season, Kenrokuen at peak, and the country's best train seat, for $1,100 less. Watch the Nov 21–23 holiday weekend crowds at the tail end.
